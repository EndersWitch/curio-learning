INTRO: An <strong>adjective</strong> describes a noun or pronoun. It tells us what kind, which one or how many. Adjectives can come before the noun or after a linking verb, and they come in several kinds.

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: What is an adjective?
CONTENT: An adjective qualifies or describes a noun or pronoun. It adds detail. It answers the questions what kind, which one and how many.
EXAMPLE: the tall, dark, handsome man<br>the loyal child<br>two clever girls
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Where adjectives go
CONTENT: An adjective can come before the noun it describes. It can also come after a linking verb such as is, am, are, was or were. The meaning is the same in both positions.
EXAMPLE: The clever boy won the prize. (before the noun)<br>The boy is clever. (after a linking verb)
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: spot_difference
TITLE: Kinds of adjective
SIDE_A: Kind
SIDE_B: Examples
CONTENT: Descriptive, what kind | loyal, intelligent, creative<br>Proper, from a proper noun | the Cape Town beaches, the August winds<br>Quantity or number | two, many, several, few, some<br>Order | first, second, last<br>Demonstrative | this, that, these, those<br>Possessive | my, his, her, our, their, its<br>Interrogative | which, what, whose
EXAMPLE: A proper adjective is a proper noun used to describe another noun, so it keeps its capital letter.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Compound adjectives and suffixes
CONTENT: Adjectives joined by a hyphen are compound adjectives. Many adjectives can be spotted or made with endings such as -ful, -less, -ous, -able, -ible, -ive, -ary, -ant and -ish. Some are made from nouns.
EXAMPLE: a well-deserved result, a feather-light cake<br>hopeful, painless, adventurous, comfortable<br>athlete → athletic
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: try_it
TITLE: Find the adjectives
CONTENT: Write the adjectives in this sentence. Two hungry learners ate the delicious pap.
EXAMPLE: Two, hungry and delicious. Two tells how many, hungry and delicious tell what kind. The is an article.
@@ENDCARD

TESTED:

Tell whether an adjective comes before the noun or after a linking verb
Name proper, quantity, demonstrative and possessive adjectives
Recognise compound adjectives

INTRO: Adjectives change form when we compare things. The <strong>positive</strong> degree describes one thing, the <strong>comparative</strong> compares two, and the <strong>superlative</strong> compares more than two. The spelling follows patterns, with a few irregular exceptions.

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: Three degrees
CONTENT: The positive degree refers to one thing. The comparative degree compares two things. The superlative degree compares more than two things and describes the best or the most.
EXAMPLE: Joan is fit. (positive)<br>Mary is fitter than Joan. (comparative)<br>Sue is the fittest of the three. (superlative)
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Spelling the degrees
CONTENT: Short adjectives add -er and -est. A short vowel before one final consonant doubles that consonant. Adjectives ending in e add -r and -st. A final y changes to i before -er and -est.
EXAMPLE: clean, cleaner, cleanest<br>thin, thinner, thinnest<br>nice, nicer, nicest<br>easy, easier, easiest<br>pretty, prettier, prettiest
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Long adjectives
CONTENT: Adjectives of more than two syllables use more and most. So do adjectives ending in -ful. Never use more with an -er ending, or most with an -est ending.
EXAMPLE: comfortable, more comfortable, most comfortable<br>interesting, more interesting, most interesting<br>beautiful, more beautiful, most beautiful
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: spot_difference
TITLE: Irregular adjectives
SIDE_A: Positive
SIDE_B: Comparative and superlative
CONTENT: good | better, best<br>bad | worse, worst<br>many, much | more, most<br>little | less, least<br>far | farther or further, farthest or furthest<br>old | older or elder, oldest or eldest
EXAMPLE: Elder and eldest are normally used for members of a family.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: common_mistake
TITLE: Double comparison
CONTENT: Do not use more with an -er form, and do not use most with an -est form. Also use the comparative for two things and the superlative for three or more.
EXAMPLE: Wrong: more taller, most easiest, the tallest of the two<br>Right: taller, easiest, the taller of the two
@@ENDCARD

TESTED:
Name the positive, comparative and superlative degrees
Spell -er and -est forms with doubling, final e and final y
Use more and most with long adjectives
Use the irregular forms good, bad, many, little, far and old
Avoid double comparisons
@@ENDLEVEL
