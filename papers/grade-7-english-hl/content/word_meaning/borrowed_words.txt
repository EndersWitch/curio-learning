INTRO: English has taken many words from other languages. These are called <strong>borrowed words</strong>. South African English has borrowed from Afrikaans and from African languages, and from languages all over the world.

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: What is a borrowed word?
CONTENT: A borrowed word is a word taken from another language and used in English. Borrowed words are usually kept with their meaning, and sometimes with their spelling. The Handbook does not cover this topic, so this level uses well-known examples.
EXAMPLE: braai from Afrikaans<br>pizza from Italian<br>karaoke from Japanese
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Why languages borrow
CONTENT: A language often borrows a word when it has no word of its own for a new thing or idea. Trade, travel and people living side by side bring languages into contact.
EXAMPLE: Karaoke came with the idea of singing along to music.<br>Indaba came from the way people met to talk.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Words from Afrikaans and African languages
CONTENT: South African English has borrowed from Afrikaans and from languages such as isiZulu and isiXhosa. Many of these words are now used by all English speakers in the country.
EXAMPLE: Afrikaans, braai, veld, trek, biltong, spoor<br>isiZulu, indaba, impala, mamba
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Words from around the world
CONTENT: Languages from many countries have given English words, often for food, music and sport.
EXAMPLE: Italian, pizza, piano, spaghetti<br>French, ballet, café, bouquet<br>Japanese, sushi, karaoke, tsunami<br>German, kindergarten<br>Hindi, shampoo, jungle, pyjamas
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: try_it
TITLE: Name the language
CONTENT: Name the language that gave English the word sushi.
EXAMPLE: Japanese.
@@ENDCARD

TESTED:

INTRO: Knowing where a word comes from helps, but you must also know what it means and how to use it. This level looks at the <strong>meanings</strong> of common borrowed words and how they are used in sentences.

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: South African borrowed words
CONTENT: These words are used in everyday South African English and each has a clear meaning.
EXAMPLE: braai, a barbecue<br>veld, open grassland<br>trek, a long trip, often a hard one<br>biltong, dried and salted meat<br>spoor, the track left by an animal<br>indaba, a meeting or discussion<br>ubuntu, the belief that people care for one another
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Words from other languages
CONTENT: These borrowed words are used worldwide and keep the meaning they had in the original language.
EXAMPLE: bouquet, a bunch of flowers<br>café, a small restaurant serving drinks and light meals<br>tsunami, a very large sea wave<br>algebra, a branch of mathematics<br>kindergarten, a school for young children
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Using the word in a sentence
CONTENT: A borrowed word is used like any other word. It can be a noun or a verb, and it follows English grammar even when its spelling is not English.
EXAMPLE: The farmers decided to trek across the veld.<br>We held a braai on Saturday.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: common_mistake
TITLE: Keep the original spelling
CONTENT: Borrowed words usually keep the spelling they have in the language they come from. Check how a word is spelt before you write it.
EXAMPLE: Wrong: bokay, kafe<br>Right: bouquet, café
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: try_it
TITLE: Use the word
CONTENT: Write a sentence using the word spoor.
EXAMPLE: The tracker followed the spoor of the leopard.
@@ENDCARD
