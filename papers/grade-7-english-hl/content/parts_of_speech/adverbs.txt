INTRO: An <strong>adverb</strong> tells us more about a verb. Adverbs of <strong>manner</strong> tell us how something is done, and adverbs of <strong>time</strong> tell us when. They can also describe adjectives and other adverbs.

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: What is an adverb?
CONTENT: An adverb modifies, or tells us more about, a verb. It can also modify an adjective, another adverb or even the whole sentence. Adverbs answer how, when, where and how often.
EXAMPLE: He ran quickly.<br>She sang beautifully.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Adverbs of manner
CONTENT: An adverb of manner tells how an action is done. Many end in -ly, but some do not. Fast and well are adverbs of manner too.
EXAMPLE: quickly, hungrily, imaginatively<br>fast, well<br>She sang beautifully.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Adverbs of time
CONTENT: An adverb of time tells when an action happens. It does not end in -ly. Some adverbs of time name a day or a moment.
EXAMPLE: today, tomorrow, yesterday, now, then, soon, immediately<br>We will leave tomorrow.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: common_mistake
TITLE: Adjective in place of an adverb
CONTENT: Use an adverb, not an adjective, to describe a verb. The Handbook warns against forms such as they spoke loud. The adverb of manner is loudly.
EXAMPLE: Wrong: They spoke loud.<br>Right: They spoke loudly.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: try_it
TITLE: Find the adverb
CONTENT: Write the adverb in this sentence and say what it tells us. Sipho walked slowly.
EXAMPLE: slowly. It tells how Sipho walked, so it is an adverb of manner.
@@ENDCARD

INTRO: Adverbs do more than describe verbs. They can modify adjectives, other adverbs and even a whole sentence. This level covers adverbs of <strong>place</strong>, <strong>degree</strong> and <strong>frequency</strong>, and how adverbs are compared.

@@CARD
TYPE: spot_difference
TITLE: Types of adverb
SIDE_A: Type
SIDE_B: Examples
CONTENT: Place, where | here, there, nowhere, up, down, home, far, near<br>Degree, to what extent | very, quite, rather, so, almost, extremely, too<br>Frequency, how often | always, never, often, seldom, usually, occasionally
EXAMPLE: Time and manner adverbs are covered in the first level. Together these make five types of adverb.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: What adverbs modify
CONTENT: An adverb can modify a verb, an adjective or another adverb. The adverb of degree very often modifies an adjective or an adverb. The Handbook gives the examples below.
EXAMPLE: She is exceptionally clever. (modifies the adjective clever)<br>Do not eat so quickly. (so modifies the adverb quickly)
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: Sentence modifiers
CONTENT: Some adverbs change the nature of the whole sentence and express the speaker's opinion. They are usually placed at the beginning of the sentence and followed by a comma. Examples are actually, certainly, fortunately, naturally, obviously, perhaps, surely and unfortunately.
EXAMPLE: Naturally, nobody owned up to the deed.<br>Perhaps you will win the lottery.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Comparing adverbs
CONTENT: Adverbs have degrees of comparison like adjectives. Short adverbs add -er and -est. Adverbs of two or more syllables use more and most. Some are irregular.
EXAMPLE: fast, faster, fastest<br>carefully, more carefully, most carefully<br>well, better, best<br>badly, worse, worst
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: try_it
TITLE: What does it modify
CONTENT: Write the word that very modifies in this sentence. The soup is very hot.
EXAMPLE: hot. Very is an adverb of degree and modifies the adjective hot.
@@ENDCARD

TITLE: Classifying an adverb
CONTENT: 1. Find the word that tells more about a verb, adjective or adverb.<br>2. Ask how, when, where, how often or to what extent.<br>3. Match the answer to the type.<br>4. Check whether it modifies the whole sentence.
EXAMPLE: He ran very quickly home.<br>Very tells how much, so degree. Quickly tells how, so manner. Home tells where, so place.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Forms and comparison
CONTENT: Use an adverb, not an adjective, to describe a verb. Well is the adverb and good is the adjective. Adverbs compare like adjectives, with the irregular forms well, better, best and badly, worse, worst.
EXAMPLE: He speaks English well.<br>She runs faster than he does.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: common_mistake
TITLE: Common adverb mistakes
CONTENT: The usual errors are using an adjective in place of an adverb, using good in place of well, and making double comparisons. Check each describing word in a sentence against these.
EXAMPLE: Wrong: He speaks English good. He runs more faster.<br>Right: He speaks English well. He runs faster.
@@ENDCARD
