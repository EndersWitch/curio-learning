INTRO: We can change the meaning of a word by adding something to it. The core of the word is the <strong>root</strong>. What comes before the root is the <strong>prefix</strong>, and what comes after it is the <strong>suffix</strong>.

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: Root, prefix and suffix
CONTENT: The core of a word is the root. A prefix comes before the root and a suffix comes after it. Adding either one can change the meaning of the word.
EXAMPLE: un + friend + ly = unfriendly<br>The prefix is un, the root is friend and the suffix is ly.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Prefixes that mean not
CONTENT: Some prefixes create an opposite, or antonym. The prefixes un, dis, in, im, il, ir, non and mis often give the idea of not or wrong.
EXAMPLE: unfriendly, dissatisfied, insufficient, improper, illiterate, irregular, non-toxic, misunderstood
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Prefixes with other meanings
CONTENT: Other prefixes add a different idea. Learn the meaning of the prefix and you can work out new words.
EXAMPLE: re, again (rewind)<br>pre, before (premature)<br>post, after (postpone)<br>sub, under (submarine)<br>anti, against (anticlockwise)<br>auto, self (autograph)<br>tele, at a distance (telephone)<br>bi, two (bicycle)<br>tri, three (tricycle)
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: common_mistake
TITLE: Keep every letter of the root
CONTENT: A prefix is joined to the root without changing it. If the prefix ends in the letter the root begins with, both letters stay.
EXAMPLE: Wrong: disatisfied, mispell, unecessary<br>Right: dissatisfied, misspell, unnecessary
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: try_it
TITLE: Build a word
CONTENT: Add a prefix that means again to the word write.
EXAMPLE: rewrite. The prefix re means again.
@@ENDCARD

TESTED:

INTRO: A <strong>suffix</strong> is a word ending. It changes the meaning of a root word and often changes its part of speech too, so a verb can become a noun and a noun can become an adjective.

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: What is a suffix?
CONTENT: A suffix is a word ending added to a root. It modifies the word and often changes the part of speech. A verb can become an abstract noun, as in rely and reliance.
EXAMPLE: kind + ness = kindness<br>care + less = careless
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Suffixes that form nouns
CONTENT: These suffixes form nouns, many of them abstract nouns. The suffixes er, or and ist usually name a person.
EXAMPLE: -ness, kindness<br>-ment, government<br>-ity, community<br>-hood, parenthood<br>-ship, friendship<br>-dom, freedom<br>-tion, completion<br>-er, -or, -ist, hairdresser, ambassador, optimist
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Suffixes that form adjectives
CONTENT: These suffixes form adjectives from other words.
EXAMPLE: -ful, full of (wonderful)<br>-less, without (homeless)<br>-ous, adventurous, dangerous<br>-able, -ible, able to be (reliable, edible)<br>-ish, like or having the nature of (selfish)<br>-ic, realistic<br>-al, practical
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Suffixes that form verbs
CONTENT: A few suffixes turn other words into verbs. The suffix ology is different, because it names the science or study of something.
EXAMPLE: -ify, beautify, purify<br>-ate, terminate, congratulate<br>-en, lengthen, enlighten<br>-ology, criminology, sociology
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: common_mistake
TITLE: Ful has one l
CONTENT: When you add the suffix full to a word it is written ful, with one l. A suffix can also change the spelling of the root, such as a final y that becomes i.
EXAMPLE: Wrong: beautifull, wonderfull<br>Right: beautiful, wonderful
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: try_it
TITLE: Add the suffix
CONTENT: Add the suffix that means without to the word home.
EXAMPLE: homeless. The suffix less means without.
@@ENDCARD

INTRO: Many words are built from one <strong>root</strong>. A word made by adding a prefix or suffix to a root is called a <strong>derivative</strong>. Knowing the root helps you work out meanings and spell new words.

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: Roots and derivatives
CONTENT: The root is the core of a word. A derivative is a word built from a root by adding a prefix, a suffix or both. Words that share a root form a word family.
EXAMPLE: friend, friendly, unfriendly, friendship, befriend<br>All of these belong to the family of friend.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: step_by_step
TITLE: Finding the root
CONTENT: 1. Take off any prefix at the start.<br>2. Take off any suffix at the end.<br>3. Fix the spelling if a letter was changed.<br>4. Check that what is left is a word that makes sense.
EXAMPLE: unhelpfulness<br>Take off un and ness, then ful, which leaves help.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Spelling changes in derivatives
CONTENT: When a suffix is added, the end of the root may change. A final y changes to i, and a final e is dropped before a vowel suffix. Keep the e after ce or ge.
EXAMPLE: heavy + ness = heaviness<br>rely + able = reliable<br>fame + ous = famous<br>service + able = serviceable
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: common_mistake
TITLE: Not every start is a prefix
CONTENT: Letters that look like a prefix are not a prefix if what remains is not a word with a related meaning. Check the root before you decide.
EXAMPLE: unlock is un + lock.<br>uncle is not un + cle.<br>real is not re + al.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: try_it
TITLE: Find the root
CONTENT: Write the root of the word disagreement.
EXAMPLE: agree. Take off dis at the start and ment at the end.
@@ENDCARD

TESTED:
Find the root of a derivative
Build derivatives from a root
Spell derivatives correctly
Recognise words in the same family
Tell true prefixes from lookalikes
@@ENDLEVEL

INTRO: Language users shorten words to save time. An <strong>abbreviation</strong> is a shortened form of a word or phrase. Some are said letter by letter, some are said as new words, and some are simply clipped short.

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: What is an abbreviation?
CONTENT: An abbreviation is a shortened form of a word or phrase, used to save time. It is usually found in writing, and when we speak we say the full word.
EXAMPLE: Mr is said as Mister.<br>Dr is said as Doctor.<br>km is said as kilometre.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Initialisms
CONTENT: When the first letters of a group of words are used, the letters are usually said one by one. Modern usage leaves out the full stops.
EXAMPLE: USA, United States of America<br>CNN, Cable News Network<br>SAA, South African Airways<br>SPCA, Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Acronyms
CONTENT: When the first letters of a group of words form a new word, it is called an acronym. The word is pronounced as it is spelt and has no full stops.
EXAMPLE: AIDS, Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome<br>UNICEF, United Nations International Children's Emergency Fund<br>NASA, National Aeronautics and Space Administration
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Clipped words
CONTENT: A clipped word is a longer word cut short. It is not a list of initials. Clipped words are common in everyday speech. The Handbook does not cover them, so they are explained here.
EXAMPLE: exam from examination<br>photo from photograph<br>maths from mathematics<br>phone from telephone
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Common abbreviations
CONTENT: Many abbreviations are used for titles, dates, times, measurements and addresses. Use the full word when you are not sure the reader knows the abbreviation.
EXAMPLE: Mr, Mrs, Dr, Prof<br>Mon, Tues, Wed, Thurs, Fri<br>Jan, Feb, Mar, Sept, Dec<br>a.m., p.m.<br>cm, km, ml<br>St, Rd, Ave
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: try_it
TITLE: Say the full word
CONTENT: Write the full words for the abbreviations in this sentence. Dr Naidoo drove 5 km on Fri.
EXAMPLE: Doctor Naidoo drove 5 kilometres on Friday.
@@ENDCARD
