INTRO: An <strong>idiom</strong> is an expression whose meaning is different from the meaning of its words, and a <strong>proverb</strong> is a short, wise saying. This Grade 7 revision checks the meanings of common ones.

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: Idioms
CONTENT: Words and expressions may be used literally or figuratively. An idiom is a figurative expression whose meaning cannot be worked out from its words. Be careful not to overuse them.
EXAMPLE: The fledglings have left the nest. (literal)<br>Their children have left the nest. (figurative, they have grown up)
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Common idioms
CONTENT: Learn the meaning of each idiom as a whole.
EXAMPLE: a bolt from the blue, a sudden and unexpected event<br>a storm in a teacup, much fuss over something unimportant<br>at a snail's pace, very slowly<br>at the eleventh hour, at the last minute<br>beat about the bush, approach a matter indirectly<br>let the cat out of the bag, reveal a secret<br>once in a blue moon, a very rare occurrence<br>rain cats and dogs, rain heavily<br>burn the candle at both ends, overwork oneself<br>kill two birds with one stone, achieve a double result with one effort
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Proverbs
CONTENT: A proverb is a short, wise saying that gives advice or states a truth. Its message applies to many situations.
EXAMPLE: Rome was not built in a day. (big undertakings take time)<br>Strike while the iron is hot. (act when the opportunity arises)<br>Strike while the iron is hot. (save for a time of need)
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: common_mistake
TITLE: Do not take idioms literally
CONTENT: If an expression makes no sense word for word, it is probably an idiom. Ask what it means as a whole, and check the context.
EXAMPLE: It is raining cats and dogs. It means it is raining very heavily, not that animals are falling.
@@ENDCARD
