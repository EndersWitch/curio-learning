LEVEL_DESC: Use subject, object and possessive pronouns correctly.

INTRO: A <strong>pronoun</strong> takes the place of a noun so that we do not repeat it. <strong>Personal pronouns</strong> change form depending on whether they are the subject or the object of a sentence. <strong>Possessive pronouns</strong> show who owns something.

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: What is a pronoun?
CONTENT: A pronoun stands in place of a noun. We use pronouns to avoid repetition. Personal pronouns refer to people or things.
EXAMPLE: Joan plays the piano. She practises it daily.<br>She stands in place of Joan and it stands in place of the piano.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: spot_difference
TITLE: Subject, object and possessive
SIDE_A: Subject and object
SIDE_B: Possessive
CONTENT: I, me | mine<br>you, you | yours<br>he, him | his<br>she, her | hers<br>we, us | ours<br>they, them | theirs
EXAMPLE: Subject pronouns do the action. Object pronouns receive it. Possessive pronouns show ownership.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Possessive pronouns or adjectives
CONTENT: Possessive pronouns stand alone and replace a noun. Possessive adjectives such as my, your, her and their come before a noun. The Handbook points out that possessive adjectives are not pronouns. Possessive pronouns never take an apostrophe.
EXAMPLE: This house was mine, but now it is ours.<br>This is my house. (my is an adjective)<br>This is theirs, not yours.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: step_by_step
TITLE: I or me
CONTENT: 1. Take out the other person.<br>2. Read the sentence as if you are alone in it.<br>3. Use the pronoun that sounds right.
EXAMPLE: Father gave the tickets to John and ___.<br>Father gave the tickets to me. So the answer is John and me.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: common_mistake
TITLE: Its and it's
CONTENT: Its with no apostrophe shows possession. It's is short for it is. The Handbook warns that these two similar words are often confused.
EXAMPLE: The bird is sitting on eggs in its nest.<br>It's getting colder by the day.
@@ENDCARD

TESTED:

Name the possessive pronouns

Know that possessive pronouns take no apostrophe
@@ENDLEVEL

LEVEL_DESC: Use this, that, these and those, and join sentences with who, whom, whose, which and that.
LEVEL_ORDER: 2

INTRO: <strong>Demonstrative pronouns</strong> point out a specific person or thing. <strong>Relative pronouns</strong> join one part of a sentence to another and take the place of a noun. Choosing between who, whom, whose, which and that is the main skill.

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: Demonstrative pronouns
CONTENT: This, that, these and those point out a specific person or thing. This and these are near, and that and those are far. If one of these words is followed by a noun, it becomes an adjective.
EXAMPLE: This is not the way we do things.<br>That has to go.<br>That picture has to go. (that describes picture, so it is an adjective here)
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: Relative pronouns
CONTENT: Relative pronouns do the job of a conjunction by joining parts of a sentence. They also replace a noun or pronoun. Who, whom and whose refer to people. That, which and what refer to animals or things.
EXAMPLE: This is my sister. She is visiting today.<br>This is my sister who is visiting today.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Who, whom and whose
CONTENT: Use who when it replaces the subject. Use whom when it replaces an object, such as her or him. Use whose when it replaces a possessive such as her or his.
EXAMPLE: This is my sister whom I am visiting today. (replaces her)<br>I felt proud of the girl whose results were excellent. (replaces her results)
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Keep it close
CONTENT: A relative pronoun should sit close to the noun it refers to. If it does not, the meaning can become unclear. When which follows a preposition, the preposition usually comes first.
EXAMPLE: I have a ring that sparkles in my jewellery box. (clear)<br>I have a ring in my jewellery box that sparkles. (unclear, does the ring or the box sparkle)
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: common_mistake
TITLE: Which for people
CONTENT: Do not use which for people. Use who, whom or whose. Using whom as a subject is also a mistake.
EXAMPLE: Wrong: The girl which won the race is my cousin.<br>Right: The girl who won the race is my cousin.
@@ENDCARD

TESTED:
Identify demonstrative pronouns
Tell a demonstrative pronoun from a demonstrative adjective
Choose who, whom, whose, which and that
Join two sentences with a relative pronoun
Place a relative pronoun close to its noun
@@ENDLEVEL

LEVEL_ORDER: 3

INTRO: <strong>Reflexive pronouns</strong> reflect an action back to the subject, and they end in <em>-self</em> or <em>-selves</em>. <strong>Interrogative pronouns</strong> are used to ask questions. This level also revisits the tricky choice between I, me and myself.

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: Reflexive pronouns
CONTENT: A reflexive pronoun refers back to the noun or pronoun that is the subject. It ends in self for one person and selves for more than one. The reflexive pronouns are myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves and themselves.
EXAMPLE: Mary sees herself in the mirror.<br>Jack cut himself with the razor.<br>We taught ourselves to swim.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: common_mistake
TITLE: Wrong reflexive forms
CONTENT: Hisself and theirselves are not correct English. The correct forms are himself and themselves. Do not use myself as a polite replacement for I or me when there is no action coming back to you.
EXAMPLE: Wrong: They enjoyed theirselves. Thabo and myself went.<br>Right: They enjoyed themselves. Thabo and I went.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: Interrogative pronouns
CONTENT: Interrogative pronouns ask questions. Who asks about a person who does something. Whom asks about a person who receives something. Whose asks about ownership. What asks about things or actions. Which asks about a choice.
EXAMPLE: Who came to tea?<br>Whose is this?<br>To whom does this belong?<br>What are you doing?<br>Which is the hotel that you recommend?
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: spot_difference
TITLE: Whose and who's
SIDE_A: Whose
SIDE_B: Who's
CONTENT: Asks about ownership | Short for who is or who has<br>Whose bag is this | Who's coming to the braai
EXAMPLE: Test by replacing the word with who is. If the sentence still makes sense, use who's. If not, use whose.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Between you and me
CONTENT: After the word between, use the object form me, not I. The Handbook gives the rule that me always follows between.
EXAMPLE: Between you and me, we shall achieve our goals.<br>The prize will be shared between you and me.
@@ENDCARD

TESTED:
Name and use reflexive pronouns
Avoid hisself, theirselves and misuse of myself
Identify interrogative pronouns
Tell whose from who's
Use me after between
@@ENDLEVEL
