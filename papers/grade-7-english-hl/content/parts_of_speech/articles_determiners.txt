INTRO: The words <strong>a</strong>, <strong>an</strong> and <strong>the</strong> are called <strong>articles</strong>. They come before nouns. Choosing between a and an depends on the sound that follows, not the spelling.

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: What are articles?
CONTENT: Articles are the words a, an and the. They usually come before a noun or before an adjective that describes the noun. The is the definite article and a and an are the indefinite articles.
EXAMPLE: The dog barked. A cat sat on a mat.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: spot_difference
TITLE: Definite and indefinite
SIDE_A: Definite (the)
SIDE_B: Indefinite (a, an)
CONTENT: A specific thing | Any one of many<br>Anne received the award for bravery. | She received an award for bravery.<br>The book on my desk | A book on a desk
EXAMPLE: Use the when both the writer and the reader know which one is meant. Use a or an for one of many.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: A or an
CONTENT: Use an before a vowel sound and a before a consonant sound. What matters is the sound, not the first letter. Some words start with a vowel letter but sound like a consonant, and some start with a consonant letter but sound like a vowel.
EXAMPLE: an umbrella, an elephant<br>a used car, a useful gadget<br>an hour, an honest man, an MG sports car
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: common_mistake
TITLE: Going by the letter
CONTENT: Learners often choose a or an by the first letter. Say the word aloud and listen to the first sound instead. Words such as university and one start with a consonant sound, and hour and honest start with a vowel sound.
EXAMPLE: Wrong: an university, a hour<br>Right: a university, an hour
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: try_it
TITLE: Choose the article
CONTENT: Write a or an. ___ European holiday, ___ egg, ___ one-rand coin.
EXAMPLE: a European holiday (starts with a y sound), an egg, a one-rand coin (starts with a w sound).
@@ENDCARD

TESTED:
Name the definite and indefinite articles
Choose a or an by the sound that follows
Use the for a specific thing
Spot article mistakes in sentences
Handle words with silent letters such as hour and honest
@@ENDLEVEL

INTRO: Articles belong to a bigger group of words called <strong>determiners</strong>. A determiner comes before a noun and tells us which one or how many. This level covers <em>this</em> and <em>that</em>, numbers, and words such as <em>many</em>, <em>much</em>, <em>few</em> and <em>each</em>.

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: Demonstratives
CONTENT: This, that, these and those point out a particular noun. This and these are for things that are near, and that and those are for things that are far. This and that go with singular nouns. These and those go with plural nouns.
EXAMPLE: This pen is mine.<br>That bakkie is old.<br>These books are heavy.<br>Those hills are far away.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Determiner or pronoun
CONTENT: When this, that, these or those comes before a noun, it describes that noun. When it stands alone in place of a noun, it is a demonstrative pronoun. The Handbook uses the example that picture to show this.
EXAMPLE: That picture has to go. (before a noun)<br>That has to go. (standing alone)
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Numbers and quantity words
CONTENT: Numerical words say exactly how many or which one in order. Quantity words give a rough amount. Many, few and fewer go with plural nouns you can count. Much, little and less go with nouns you cannot count. Each and every go with a singular noun.
EXAMPLE: one book, second place, single file<br>many learners, few learners, fewer learners<br>much water, little sugar, less time<br>Each learner has a pen.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: example
TITLE: More quantity words
CONTENT: Some quantity words work with both kinds of noun, such as all, some, most, no, any, more. Both, either and neither refer to two things. Another and other point to something extra.
EXAMPLE: all learners, all water<br>some books, some rice<br>both hands, either road, neither answer<br>another chance, other options
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: common_mistake
TITLE: Much and many mix-ups
CONTENT: Do not use much with plural nouns you can count, and do not use many with nouns you cannot count. Use fewer for countable plurals and less for uncountable nouns.
EXAMPLE: Wrong: much learners, less cars, many water<br>Right: many learners, fewer cars, much water
@@ENDCARD

TESTED:
Use this, that, these and those with the right nouns
Tell a demonstrative determiner from a demonstrative pronoun
Identify numerical determiners
Choose between much and many, less and fewer
Use each and every with singular nouns
@@ENDLEVEL
