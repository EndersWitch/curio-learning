LEVEL_DESC: Tell common nouns from proper nouns and use capital letters correctly.

INTRO: A <strong>noun</strong> is a naming word. <strong>Common nouns</strong> name ordinary things such as table, teacher and city. <strong>Proper nouns</strong> name particular people, places and times, and they always start with a capital letter.

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: What is a noun?
CONTENT: A noun is a naming word. Every person, place or thing has a name. A common noun names ordinary, everyday things. It does not take a capital letter unless it starts a sentence.
EXAMPLE: There are desks and chairs in every classroom.<br>the sea, a fly, an orange
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Proper nouns take capitals
CONTENT: A proper noun names a particular person, place, day, month, holiday, language or school subject. It always begins with a capital letter. When a common noun is part of a name, it takes a capital letter too.
EXAMPLE: Thabo, Professor Jones, Durban, Tuesday, December, Human Rights Day<br>English, History<br>Saunders Street, the Danube River
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: spot_difference
TITLE: Common and proper nouns
SIDE_A: Common noun
SIDE_B: Proper noun
CONTENT: girl | Naledi<br>city | Johannesburg<br>river | Limpopo River<br>day | Monday<br>language | Zulu
EXAMPLE: The common noun names the kind of thing. The proper noun names one particular example.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: common_mistake
TITLE: Capitals in the wrong place
CONTENT: Do not give a capital letter to a common noun in the middle of a sentence. Do not forget the capital on a name, a day or a month either.
EXAMPLE: Wrong: The Learners wrote a test on friday.<br>Right: The learners wrote a test on Friday.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: try_it
TITLE: Add the capitals
CONTENT: Rewrite correctly. my friend lerato lives in cape town.
EXAMPLE: My friend Lerato lives in Cape Town. The sentence starts with a capital, and Lerato and Cape Town are proper nouns.
@@ENDCARD

LEVEL_DESC: Form plural nouns correctly and show ownership with apostrophes.
LEVEL_ORDER: 2

QUESTION_COUNT: 9
INTRO: Most nouns have a <strong>singular</strong> form for one and a <strong>plural</strong> form for more than one. The spelling changes follow patterns, and a few nouns break them. A <strong>possessive noun</strong> shows who owns something, and the apostrophe goes in a different place for one owner and for many.

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Regular plural patterns
CONTENT: Most nouns just add -s. Nouns ending in ch, sh, s, ss or x add -es. A consonant followed by y changes to -ies, but a vowel followed by y just adds -s. Many nouns ending in f or fe change to -ves.
EXAMPLE: house → houses<br>church → churches, box → boxes<br>baby → babies, monkey → monkeys<br>knife → knives, shelf → shelves<br>roof → roofs, chief → chiefs
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Nouns ending in o and special plurals
CONTENT: Many nouns ending in o add -es, but musical and shortened words simply add -s. Some nouns change inside, and some stay the same in the plural. A few only exist in the plural.
EXAMPLE: potato → potatoes, hero → heroes<br>piano → pianos, photo → photos<br>child → children, man → men, tooth → teeth, mouse → mice<br>sheep, deer, fish stay the same<br>scissors, trousers, clothes are always plural
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Showing ownership
CONTENT: For one owner, add an apostrophe and s. For a plural ending in s, add only an apostrophe. For a plural that does not end in s, add an apostrophe and s. The Handbook suggests covering the apostrophe with a finger to check what comes before it.
EXAMPLE: the girl's diary (one girl)<br>the girls' diaries (many girls)<br>the children's toys
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: common_mistake
TITLE: No apostrophe for plurals
CONTENT: Never use an apostrophe and s just to make a plural. An apostrophe is for ownership or for a contraction.
EXAMPLE: Wrong: There are many girl's in my class.<br>Right: There are many girls in my class.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: try_it
TITLE: Plural and possessive
CONTENT: Write the plural of country and then the possessive form for the toys that belong to many children.
EXAMPLE: countries (consonant plus y changes to ies). The children's toys (children is plural but does not end in s, so add 's).
@@ENDCARD

TESTED:
Form plurals for nouns ending in ch, sh, s, x, y, f, fe and o
Use irregular plurals such as children, teeth and mice
Recognise nouns that do not change or are always plural
Place the apostrophe for one owner and for many owners
Avoid apostrophes in simple plurals
@@ENDLEVEL

INTRO: A <strong>concrete noun</strong> names something you can see or touch. An <strong>abstract noun</strong> names an idea or feeling, such as courage or friendship. A <strong>collective noun</strong> names a group, such as a herd of cattle.

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: Abstract and concrete nouns
CONTENT: An abstract noun names something we cannot see, touch or measure, such as a feeling or an idea. A concrete noun names something we can see or touch. The Handbook test is that you can give an abstract noun to someone, but not in a box.
EXAMPLE: Abstract: love, kindness, courage, friendship, freedom<br>Concrete: ball, chair, bakkie, river
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Spotting abstract nouns
CONTENT: Many abstract nouns end in -ness, -ship, -hood, -ment, -tion, -ence, -ance, -age or -ism. They are often made from adjectives or verbs. A useful test is to see whether you can say he is full of the noun.
EXAMPLE: kind → kindness<br>friend → friendship<br>imagine → imagination<br>He is full of courage.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: Collective nouns
CONTENT: A collective noun names a group of people, animals or things. It is a more exact way of saying lots of. Each group has its own name, and some groups can use more than one.
EXAMPLE: a herd of cattle<br>a pride of lions<br>a swarm of bees<br>a fleet of ships<br>a bunch of keys<br>a litter of puppies<br>a gaggle of geese
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: common_mistake
TITLE: Nouns ending in -ing
CONTENT: The Handbook points out that a noun ending in -ing is not an abstract noun. It is called a verbal noun or gerund, because it comes from a verb. Look at what the word means in the sentence before you decide.
EXAMPLE: Swimming is good exercise. (a verbal noun, not an abstract noun)<br>Kindness is a gift. (an abstract noun)
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: try_it
TITLE: Find the abstract noun
CONTENT: Write the abstract noun in this sentence. The learners showed great respect to the principal.
EXAMPLE: respect. It is a feeling you cannot see or touch. Learners and principal are concrete nouns.
@@ENDCARD

TESTED:
Tell abstract nouns from concrete nouns
Use the suffixes -ness, -ship, -ment and -tion to spot abstract nouns
Make abstract nouns from adjectives and verbs
Choose the correct collective noun for a group
Know that nouns ending in -ing are verbal nouns
@@ENDLEVEL

INTRO: A <strong>compound noun</strong> joins two words into one name, such as <em>bookshelf</em>. A <strong>complex noun</strong> is a noun with extra words attached to it. Nouns also have <strong>gender</strong>, and the plurals of compound nouns follow their own rules.

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: Compound nouns
CONTENT: A compound noun is two words that together name one thing. It can be written as one word, with a hyphen, or as two words. The Handbook gives hand plus bag as handbag and book plus shelf as bookshelf.
EXAMPLE: handbag, bookshelf, rainbow<br>child-lock, sister-in-law<br>parking bay
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Plurals of compound nouns
CONTENT: In most compound nouns the last word takes the plural. When the noun contains a preposition, the word before the preposition takes the plural. Hyphenated words follow the same idea.
EXAMPLE: handbag → handbags<br>bookshelf → bookshelves<br>sister-in-law → sisters-in-law<br>runner-up → runners-up<br>passer-by → passers-by
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: Complex nouns
CONTENT: A complex noun is a noun with other words attached that describe or identify it, such as adjectives, a phrase or a clause. The whole group of words works as one noun. This idea comes from the CAPS curriculum, which gives the examples below.
EXAMPLE: a very big tree<br>the dog that bites<br>The University of the Witwatersrand
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: spot_difference
TITLE: Four genders
SIDE_A: Gender
SIDE_B: Examples
CONTENT: Masculine | prince, son, brother, king<br>Feminine | queen, daughter, sister, mother<br>Common | teacher, doctor, learner, friend<br>Neuter | book, engine, computer
EXAMPLE: Common gender can be either male or female. Many job titles are common gender today.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: common_mistake
TITLE: Calling things she or he
CONTENT: Neuter nouns are things, but writers sometimes give them gender for effect, especially ships, the sun and the moon. This is a deliberate choice and is called personification. Do not assume a job title is masculine or feminine.
EXAMPLE: The Titanic embarked on her maiden voyage.<br>The moon entered her last phase.
@@ENDCARD

TESTED:

Form the plurals of compound and hyphenated nouns
Recognise a complex noun

@@ENDLEVEL

TITLE: Plurals and apostrophes
CONTENT: Plural nouns follow spelling patterns, and an apostrophe is never used just to make a plural. One owner takes 's. A plural ending in s takes only an apostrophe. A plural that does not end in s takes 's.
EXAMPLE: babies, knives, potatoes<br>the girl's diary, the girls' diaries, the children's toys
@@ENDCARD

TYPE: common_mistake
TITLE: Common noun mistakes
CONTENT: The most common errors are capitals on common nouns, missing capitals on names, wrong plural endings and apostrophes in plurals. Check each noun in a passage against these four.
EXAMPLE: Wrong: Two teacher's visited johannesburg.<br>Right: Two teachers visited Johannesburg.
