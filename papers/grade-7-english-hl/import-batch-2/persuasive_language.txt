INTRO: Writers and speakers use language to <strong>persuade</strong> us. To judge what we read, we need to tell a <strong>fact</strong> from an <strong>opinion</strong> and notice <strong>emotive</strong> words that aim at our feelings.

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: Emotive language
CONTENT: Emotive language appeals to the emotions rather than to the intellect. Its words are chosen to make the reader feel something, such as pity, anger or excitement.
EXAMPLE: The heartless storm crushed the helpless village.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: spot_difference
TITLE: Fact and opinion
SIDE_A: Kind
SIDE_B: Example
CONTENT: Fact, definitely true | The Nile is the longest river in Africa.<br>Opinion, based on views or feelings | Shakespeare is extremely interesting to read.
EXAMPLE: A fact can be checked. An opinion is a view that other people may not share.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Subjective and objective
CONTENT: Subjective writing is personal and biased, a one-sided point of view. Objective writing is fair and unbiased, takes all sides into account and is not influenced by personal feelings.
EXAMPLE: I think she has passed with flying colours. (subjective)<br>Based on her marks, she has passed with flying colours. (objective)
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: common_mistake
TITLE: Strong words are not facts
CONTENT: A sentence is not a fact just because it sounds sure of itself. Words such as best, worst, terrible and wonderful usually signal an opinion.
EXAMPLE: Wrong: Winter is the best season is a fact.<br>Right: Winter is the best season is an opinion.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: try_it
TITLE: Fact or opinion
CONTENT: Say whether this sentence is a fact or an opinion. Table Mountain is the most beautiful mountain in the world.
EXAMPLE: Opinion. Most beautiful is a personal view that cannot be checked.
@@ENDCARD

TESTED:
Tell a fact from an opinion
Recognise emotive language
Tell subjective from objective writing
Spot words that signal opinion
Find sentences that mix fact and opinion
@@ENDLEVEL

INTRO: Speakers and writers use <strong>techniques</strong> to convince us. This level covers rhetorical questions, repetition, exaggeration, generalisation, propaganda and sensationalism.

@@CARD
TYPE: definition
TITLE: Rhetorical questions
CONTENT: A rhetorical question expects no answer. The speaker uses it to make a point and to strengthen an argument, and it makes the audience stop and think about the question.
EXAMPLE: Who doesn't want a safer school?<br>Why are we allowing stress to become an invisible enemy?
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Other persuasive techniques
CONTENT: Persuasive writers often use emotive language, repetition for emphasis, exaggeration, and generalisations that claim too much.
EXAMPLE: We must act now. We must act together. (repetition)<br>The greatest snack in the history of the universe. (exaggeration)<br>Everyone knows that homework is a waste of time. (generalisation)
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Propaganda
CONTENT: Propaganda aims to persuade large audiences with subjective and biased ideas. Evidence is distorted, opinions are stated as facts, and the facts are chosen selectively.
EXAMPLE: Techniques include emotive language, exaggeration, generalisations and rhetorical questions.
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: key_rule
TITLE: Sensationalism
CONTENT: Sensationalism is the deliberate use of dramatic words to excite, horrify or capture interest. It is common in headlines.
EXAMPLE: SHOCKING STORM RIPS THROUGH TOWN versus Storm causes damage in town
@@ENDCARD

@@CARD
TYPE: try_it
TITLE: Name the technique
CONTENT: Name the technique in the sentence Surely nobody wants dirty beaches.
EXAMPLE: A rhetorical question, since no answer is expected.
@@ENDCARD
